Course Description

ENGR 1050 is a first-year introduction to scientific computing for engineers. The course assumes no prior programming background. We start from Python fundamentals (variables, control flow, functions, classes), move to numerical methods and microcontroller labs built around a Raspberry Pi Pico and a small helicopter testbed, and end with a unit on data science and AI for engineering. The semester is organized in three units, each capped by an in-class pencil-and-paper exam, and concludes with a final project. In the final project, students will be given a budget to purchase mechatronic components and invent a small system; instead of a final exam, we will hold a showcase of all projects.

The course meets twice per week. Most meetings combine a short lecture with live coding in a Jupyter notebook. Several meetings are dedicated lab sessions in the GM lab in the basement of Towne, where we will work hands-on with Raspberry Pi Picos and learn how to use them to drive sensors, motors, and simple mechatronic devices.

Extras: extra practice

All optional. If a lecture moved too fast or you want more reps before an exam, start with the interactive sites below - they drill the fundamentals with immediate feedback, and neither requires an account.

Below those is a short companion textbook that walks through Python and numpy syntax with worked examples. Each chapter has a written page and a runnable Colab notebook with every example as a live cell.

Course Policies

Late Policy

Late submissions are not accepted without documentation. The lowest homework and lowest two in-class exercise grades are dropped to absorb missed work. Exam and final project grades are not dropped.

AI Use

This course teaches AI-assisted coding as part of the curriculum (Section 3). Use of tools like ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot is encouraged for learning and exploration outside of exams. There are two requirements:

  • All submitted work must be your own. If you used an AI tool, cite which tool and describe what you used it for.
  • Be vigilant about overestimating your command of the material. The pencil-and-paper exams are designed to make sure you can solve problems without an LLM. If your homework performance does not match your exam performance, that is a signal that you are leaning too heavily on AI.

Direct copying of solutions from AI or the internet without understanding is considered academic dishonesty. If we ask you to explain why you wrote code a particular way and you cannot, that is a problem.

Collaboration

You are encouraged to discuss the material with classmates. The final product must be your own work and you must acknowledge any collaboration.
